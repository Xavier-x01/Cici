## Exhibit Summary

This chapter examines how Dostoevsky's fiction diagnosed the spiritual fractures of nineteenth-century Russian civilization — the tension between Western rationalism and the Eastern Orthodox soul, between the individualist underground man and communal peasant faith. The exhibit argues that literature in this case is not mere illustration: Dostoevsky was producing real-time civilizational analysis at the moment of maximum pressure between modernization and tradition. Seven artifacts trace the world he was born into, the texts he produced, and the caution any reader must carry when using fiction as historical evidence.

## Curatorial Note

**Selection rationale:**
The Orthodox chant opens the exhibit because it precedes and outlasts Dostoevsky — it is the civilizational substrate he was analyzing, not his invention. A visitor needs to hear that sound before encountering the man. The Perov portrait and St. Petersburg map establish person and city before engaging the texts. The three primary-room artifacts (Notes from Underground excerpt, Christ Pantocrator icon, manuscript draft page) triangulate the chapter's argument from three angles: the rational-underground voice, the spiritual tradition it reacts against, and the physical act of composition. The caution artifact closes the exhibit because literature-as-history is the methodological risk the entire chapter rests on.

**Cultural judgment:**
This exhibit takes seriously the interpretive claim that Dostoevsky was conducting civilizational diagnosis, not merely writing novels. That reading is contested — literary scholars resist reducing fiction to historical data. The exhibit presents the artifacts without forcing that conclusion; the visitor should reach it, or reject it, independently.

**Limits:**
Dostoevsky's fiction cannot be treated as sociological data about nineteenth-century Russia. The underground man is a constructed voice, not a survey respondent. This exhibit does not claim Dostoevsky described Russian reality — it claims he identified a structural tension that independent historical sources confirm. Those sources are the chapter's burden of proof, not the novels themselves.
