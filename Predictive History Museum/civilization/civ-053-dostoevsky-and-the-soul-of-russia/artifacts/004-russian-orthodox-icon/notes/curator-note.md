# Curator Note: Icon of Christ Pantocrator

**Artifact:** civ-053-004
**Room:** primary_artifacts_and_texts
**Date of note:** 2026-05-18

## Why This Artifact

The icon is the other half of the exhibit's central tension: the chant gave it sound; the icon gives it a face. Together they constitute the tradition the underground man is reacting against.

Placed in primary_artifacts_and_texts alongside the Notes from Underground excerpt, the icon asks the visitor to hold both objects at once — the rational-resentful voice, and the image that tradition held up as the answer to exactly that kind of suffering. The juxtaposition is the exhibit's argument.

## Selection Notes

The Christ Pantocrator type is appropriate because it is the most widely reproduced and recognized icon in the Orthodox tradition, and because Dostoevsky explicitly engages the Pantocrator type in *The Brothers Karamazov*. The specific image selected (Sinai, 6th century) is well-known but not specifically Russian.

**Decision needed before finalizing:** A Novgorod School Pantocrator would be more accurate to the chapter's focus on Russian civilization specifically. The Tretyakov Gallery and Russian Museum both hold notable examples. Curator should select a specifically Russian icon if a suitable public domain image is available.

## Limits

No icon can speak for the full range of Orthodox experience. Icons were objects of veneration in specific liturgical contexts — removing one from that context for exhibit purposes changes its meaning. The exhibit should acknowledge this displacement in the wall label.
