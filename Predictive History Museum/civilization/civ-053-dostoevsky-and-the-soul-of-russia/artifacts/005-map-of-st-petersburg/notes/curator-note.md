# Curator Note: Map of St. Petersburg ca. 1860s

**Artifact:** civ-053-005
**Room:** context_room
**Date of note:** 2026-05-18

## Why This Artifact

St. Petersburg is not simply where Dostoevsky lived — it is the spatial embodiment of the civilizational argument this chapter makes. The city was built by Peter the Great as a deliberate rupture with Russian tradition, a European capital implanted on Russian soil. Understanding the city is understanding the pressure system.

The map grounds abstract civilizational claims in specific geography. After the chant and the portrait, the visitor now sees where that man lived and what kind of city surrounded him.

## Selection Notes

The Illustrated London News engraving was chosen for availability and public domain status. It represents a contemporary Western view of the city, which has its own interpretive value (this is how Europe saw Russia's European city).

An alternative worth considering: a Russian-produced city plan of the period, which would show St. Petersburg's own self-image — the regular grid, the imperial monuments, the river crossings. A Russian plan would better represent the city's self-construction as European; the London News view represents European confirmation of that construction.

## Limits

No single image captures the full geography of Dostoevsky's St. Petersburg. The underground man's basement, the streets of *Crime and Punishment*, the districts where poverty concentrated — these are specific micro-geographies that a city overview cannot convey. The map establishes macro-context, not the lived street-level experience.
