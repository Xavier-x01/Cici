## Exhibit Summary

This chapter examines how crisis pressure operates in real time — the shape of its cascades, the false plateaus that precede secondary collapses, and the pattern of institutional response under maximum stress. Using the Great Depression and the Panic of 1907 as primary cases, the chapter argues that crisis pressure is not a single event but a pressure system with a characteristic signature: it does not resolve cleanly, and the temptation to read temporary relief as recovery is one of its most dangerous features. The exhibit closes with an explicit caution against treating historical crisis patterns as templates for predicting future ones.

## Curatorial Note

**Selection rationale:**
The unemployment chart opens because it establishes that crisis pressure is quantitative and measurable before any narrative begins. The WWII rationing poster places pressure in its downstream consequence — crisis does not end at trough; it persists into the mobilization that follows. The 1907 newspaper and FDR transcript are primary documents from different decades, showing how the same pressure pattern recurs. The pressure-cascade diagram is a curator-authored analytical tool — it makes the structural argument visible where documents cannot. The caution artifact closes because pattern-matching from historical crises to future ones is the chapter's exact methodological risk.

**Cultural judgment:**
The exhibit uses exclusively American examples for the primary cases. This is a limitation the curator has accepted for coherence, not universality. The chapter's argument about crisis pressure system signatures is meant to be general; the American cases are the most thoroughly documented. Visitors should not conclude that the pattern is American or that non-American crises differ in structure.

**Limits:**
No historical crisis is identical to another. The pressure signature exhibited here (the 1929–1940 curve, the 1907 panic) cannot be used to predict when or how a future crisis will cascade. The chapter identifies pattern family resemblances, not predictive laws.
