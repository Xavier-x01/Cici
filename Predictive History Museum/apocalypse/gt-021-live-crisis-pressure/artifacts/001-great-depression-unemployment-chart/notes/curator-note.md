# Curator Note: U.S. Unemployment Rate 1929–1940

**Artifact:** gt-021-001
**Room:** entrance_artifact
**Date of note:** 2026-05-18

## Why This Artifact

The chart is the exhibit's opening statement because it establishes that crisis pressure is a measurable phenomenon with a characteristic shape before any interpretation begins. Visitors who encounter the documents and speeches first are tempted to treat the Depression as a narrative about specific decisions. The chart prevents that: it shows the structural shape first.

The 1937–1938 secondary collapse is specifically important to the chapter's argument. Most popular accounts of the Depression focus on the 1929–1933 descent and the New Deal response. The recession within the Depression — partial recovery followed by renewed collapse — is the chapter's evidence that crisis pressure does not resolve along simple trajectories.

## Selection Notes

The Wikimedia Commons BLS visualization is the standard reference chart for this period. SVG format is preferable for display — it scales without loss and the data is embedded in the file.

Note: the BLS historical unemployment series uses a definition of unemployment that differs from modern BLS methodology. The chart is accurate to the data as measured at the time; comparisons to modern unemployment rates require methodological adjustment.

## Limits

The chart shows a national aggregate. It does not show regional variation (unemployment was much higher in manufacturing regions), racial disparity (Black unemployment was significantly higher), or sector differences. The national aggregate is the right starting point but should not be treated as uniform experience.
